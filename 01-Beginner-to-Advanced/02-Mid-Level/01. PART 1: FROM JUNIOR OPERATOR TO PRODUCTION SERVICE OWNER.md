# PART 1: FROM JUNIOR OPERATOR TO PRODUCTION SERVICE OWNER

## Goal

Understand the transition from completing assigned operational tasks to owning the technical health and delivery lifecycle of a production service.

A Junior engineer is commonly trusted to work within an established system. The architecture already exists. Deployment procedures exist. Access boundaries have been defined. More experienced engineers are available for decisions that exceed the Junior engineer's authority or experience.

At Mid-Level, the responsibility changes.

You are increasingly expected to understand the service as a complete production system, make bounded technical decisions independently, recognize risk before making changes, investigate failures across multiple layers, and improve the service rather than simply operate it.

The transition can be represented as:

### Junior

```text
Task
↓
Procedure
↓
Execute
↓
Verify
↓
Escalate
```

### Mid-Level

```text
Requirement / Problem
↓
Understand System
↓
Assess Risk
↓
Choose Implementation
↓
Execute
↓
Verify
↓
Observe
↓
Improve
```

The difference is not simply that a Mid-Level engineer knows more commands or tools.

The difference is **engineering ownership**.

A production service is not only the application process that serves requests. It is the complete system required to build, configure, deploy, operate, observe, secure, troubleshoot, recover, and change that application safely.

A useful starting model is:

```text
Source
↓
Build
↓
Artifact
↓
Configuration
↓
Infrastructure
↓
Deployment
↓
Runtime
↓
Network
↓
Dependencies
↓
Application
↓
Users
```

Around this delivery and runtime path are additional operational responsibilities:

```text
Security
Observability
Reliability
Performance
Documentation
Recovery
Change Management
Incident Response
```

Mid-Level service ownership means learning to reason across these relationships instead of treating each technology as an isolated tool.

---

# 1. THE CHANGE IN ENGINEERING RESPONSIBILITY

Consider a request:

> Deploy version 2.4.1 of the application to the staging environment.

A Junior engineer may receive:

- the repository
- deployment instructions
- environment details
- approved version
- required credentials
- verification procedure
- rollback procedure

The engineering workflow may therefore be:

```text
Receive Task
↓
Confirm Instructions
↓
Perform Deployment
↓
Run Verification
↓
Report Result
↓
Escalate Unexpected Behavior
```

This is legitimate engineering work.

The change at Mid-Level is that the task may instead arrive as:

> We need to deploy this service reliably to the staging and production environments.

Now several decisions may not already have answers.

You may need to determine:

- how the artifact is built
- how versions are identified
- where configuration comes from
- how secrets are supplied
- what infrastructure is required
- how the service starts
- how traffic reaches it
- what dependencies must be available
- what health means
- how deployment is verified
- what should be monitored
- what happens when deployment fails
- how rollback works
- what operational documentation is required

The problem has changed from:

```text
How do I execute this procedure?
```

to:

```text
What procedure should safely solve this requirement?
```

That transition is central to Mid-Level engineering.

---

# 2. EXECUTION RESPONSIBILITY

Execution responsibility means being responsible for correctly performing an established action.

Examples include:

- restarting an approved service
- applying a reviewed Terraform plan
- deploying an approved application version
- executing an established rollback
- adding an approved configuration value
- following an incident runbook
- scaling a workload according to an approved procedure

The important characteristics are:

```text
Known Task
+
Known Procedure
+
Known Boundaries
+
Known Verification
```

Execution responsibility remains important at every engineering level.

Senior engineers still execute commands.

Architects still review implementation details.

The distinction is not whether someone performs operational work.

The distinction is how much of the **reasoning, design, risk assessment, and ownership** they are expected to provide.

---

# 3. ENGINEERING OWNERSHIP

Engineering ownership means being able to take a requirement or problem and reason about the system necessary to address it.

That includes questions such as:

```text
What exactly is required?

What already exists?

Which components are involved?

Which assumptions am I making?

What could fail?

What is the safest implementation?

How will I test it?

How will I deploy it?

How will I verify it?

How will I observe it afterward?

How will I recover if it fails?

What should be improved afterward?
```

This does not mean acting without review.

Ownership and authority are different.

You may own the technical investigation while still requiring approval to perform a production change.

You may own a service while another team owns its database.

You may recommend an infrastructure change while a platform team controls the infrastructure account.

You may identify a security weakness while a security team owns the organization-wide policy.

A Mid-Level engineer must therefore understand both:

```text
What am I responsible for?
```

and:

```text
What am I authorized to change?
```

---

# 4. OWNERSHIP DOES NOT MEAN DOING EVERYTHING YOURSELF

Production services commonly cross multiple engineering boundaries.

A service may depend on:

```text
Application Team
      ↓
CI/CD Platform
      ↓
Artifact Registry
      ↓
Cloud Infrastructure
      ↓
Container Platform
      ↓
Network
      ↓
Database
      ↓
Identity Platform
      ↓
External Provider
```

No single engineer necessarily administers every layer.

Service ownership therefore does not mean:

> I personally control every component.

It means:

> I understand the service well enough to identify the relevant component, collect useful evidence, understand the dependency relationship, involve the correct owner when necessary, and continue the investigation across ownership boundaries.

This distinction becomes extremely important during incidents.

---

# 5. KNOWING THE SERVICE

You cannot meaningfully own a production service that you cannot explain.

For every service you operate, you should eventually be able to answer:

- What does the service do?
- Who or what uses it?
- Where is its source code?
- How is it built?
- What artifact is deployed?
- Where is that artifact stored?
- Where does the service run?
- What runtime does it require?
- What configuration does it require?
- Which values are sensitive?
- Which ports does it use?
- How does traffic reach it?
- What does it call?
- What calls it?
- What data does it store?
- What happens if a dependency fails?
- How is the service monitored?
- How is health determined?
- How is it deployed?
- How is it rolled back?
- How is it recovered?
- Where are the operational instructions?

If several of these questions cannot be answered, that is itself useful information.

It reveals gaps in service knowledge or documentation.

---

# 6. A SERVICE IS MORE THAN SOURCE CODE

A common mistake is to think:

```text
Repository
=
Service
```

The repository may contain the application's source code, but the production service is larger.

Consider:

```text
Source Repository
        ↓
Build System
        ↓
Artifact
        ↓
Artifact Registry
        ↓
Deployment System
        ↓
Infrastructure
        ↓
Runtime
        ↓
Application Process
        ↓
Network Endpoint
        ↓
Dependencies
        ↓
User
```

A failure anywhere in this chain can affect the service.

The source code can be perfectly correct while production is unavailable because:

- DNS is wrong
- a certificate expired
- a secret is inaccessible
- the deployment used the wrong image
- the database is unavailable
- a firewall rule blocks traffic
- the process cannot bind its port
- the disk is full
- the container is repeatedly restarting
- the load balancer has no healthy targets
- a dependency is timing out

Service ownership requires thinking beyond the repository.

---

# 7. SOURCE OWNERSHIP

Understanding the source does not mean every DevOps engineer must become the primary application developer.

It means understanding enough about the repository to operate the service safely.

You should know:

- repository location
- default branch
- important directories
- application entry point
- dependency definitions
- test locations
- build files
- container definitions
- infrastructure code
- pipeline definitions
- deployment manifests
- configuration templates
- operational documentation

You should also understand how a source change moves toward production.

For example:

```text
Developer Change
↓
Commit
↓
Pull Request
↓
Review
↓
Automated Validation
↓
Merge
↓
Build
↓
Artifact
↓
Deployment
↓
Verification
```

If the service fails after deployment, this path becomes part of the investigation.

---

# 8. BUILD OWNERSHIP

A service owner should understand how source becomes something deployable.

Depending on the application, the build may produce:

- binary
- package
- archive
- container image
- static files
- language-specific artifact

The exact mechanism varies.

The important questions remain:

```text
What is built?

From which source revision?

Using which dependencies?

Using which build environment?

What identifies the resulting artifact?

Where is it stored?

How do we know what reached production?
```

A deployment that cannot be traced back to a source revision creates operational uncertainty.

---

# 9. ARTIFACT IDENTITY

Suppose production is failing.

The first question should not be:

> What version should production be running?

It should be possible to determine:

> What version is production actually running?

A useful traceability chain is:

```text
Source Commit
↓
Build
↓
Artifact Identity
↓
Deployment
↓
Running Version
```

The exact identifiers vary.

Examples may include:

- Git commit
- release version
- package version
- container image tag
- container image digest

The operational requirement is traceability.

---

# 10. CONFIGURATION OWNERSHIP

Applications rarely operate entirely from compiled or packaged code.

They also require configuration.

Examples include:

- environment
- port
- feature flags
- dependency addresses
- timeouts
- log level
- worker count
- database connection information
- queue configuration
- external API endpoints

Configuration can alter application behavior without changing source code.

Therefore:

```text
Same Artifact
+
Different Configuration
=
Potentially Different Runtime Behavior
```

A Mid-Level engineer must know where important configuration comes from and how it reaches the running workload.

---

# 11. CONFIGURATION SOURCE OF TRUTH

Configuration may originate from:

- repository files
- environment variables
- configuration management
- deployment manifests
- cloud configuration systems
- Kubernetes ConfigMaps
- secret stores
- platform settings
- runtime flags

The exact mechanism is less important than being able to answer:

```text
Where is the authoritative value defined?

How does it reach the application?

Who can change it?

How is the change reviewed?

How is the effective runtime value verified?
```

A configuration value existing in Git does not prove the running application is using it.

---

# 12. SECRETS ARE CONFIGURATION WITH DIFFERENT RISK

Some configuration is sensitive.

Examples include:

- passwords
- API tokens
- private keys
- signing material
- credentials

These values require stronger controls.

A service map should identify:

```text
Service
↓
Secret Requirement
↓
Secret Source
↓
Authorized Workload
```

It should not expose the secret value itself.

Operational documentation should explain how the secret is supplied and rotated without publishing the credential.

---

# 13. DEPLOYMENT OWNERSHIP

A service owner should understand how an artifact moves into an environment.

That includes:

- trigger
- pipeline
- artifact
- target environment
- permissions
- deployment mechanism
- health verification
- failure behavior
- rollback or recovery

A deployment should not be treated as a mysterious pipeline button.

The engineer should understand the major state transitions.

---

# 14. DEPLOYMENT IS NOT SERVICE HEALTH

Suppose a pipeline reports:

```text
Deployment Job: SUCCESS
```

That proves something about the deployment process.

It does not necessarily prove:

```text
Application: HEALTHY
```

The deployment system may have successfully:

- copied files
- updated a manifest
- replaced containers
- applied infrastructure configuration
- started a process

while the application still fails to serve users.

Therefore:

```text
Deployment Completed
↓
Runtime Started
↓
Health Verified
↓
Traffic Verified
↓
Dependencies Verified
↓
Service Observed
```

are separate facts.

---

# 15. INFRASTRUCTURE OWNERSHIP

A production service runs somewhere.

Examples include:

- virtual machines
- containers
- Kubernetes
- serverless platforms
- managed application platforms

The service owner should understand enough infrastructure to answer:

- where does the service run?
- which environment?
- which region or location?
- what compute resources exist?
- which network contains them?
- what storage is attached?
- what identity does the workload use?
- which infrastructure is dedicated?
- which infrastructure is shared?
- how is infrastructure changed?
- which team owns the platform?

Infrastructure ownership does not necessarily mean direct administrative access.

It means understanding the infrastructure dependency.

---

# 16. RUNTIME OWNERSHIP

A deployment eventually becomes a running workload.

That may be:

```text
systemd service
```

or:

```text
container
```

or:

```text
Kubernetes Pod
```

or another platform-managed runtime.

The service owner must understand the path:

```text
Artifact
↓
Runtime
↓
Process
↓
Listener
↓
Application
```

This becomes critical during troubleshooting.

---

# 17. DEPENDENCY OWNERSHIP

Most production services are not isolated.

They depend on other systems.

Examples include:

- database
- cache
- queue
- object storage
- identity provider
- payment provider
- internal API
- DNS
- certificate service
- external SaaS API

A service can be healthy internally while still failing because a dependency is unavailable.

Therefore service ownership requires dependency awareness.

---

# 18. SERVICE BOUNDARIES

A service boundary defines what belongs to the service and what exists outside it.

Consider:

```text
Client
↓
API Service
↓
Database
```

If the API team owns the application but a database team owns the database, the operational boundary may be:

```text
API Team Responsibility
┌───────────────────────┐
│ Application           │
│ Deployment            │
│ Application Config    │
│ Application Metrics   │
└───────────────────────┘
           ↓
     Dependency Boundary
           ↓
┌───────────────────────┐
│ Managed Database      │
│ Database Platform     │
└───────────────────────┘
Database Team Responsibility
```

The API team still needs to understand how database health affects the API.

Ownership boundaries do not remove dependency responsibility.

---

# 19. UPSTREAM AND DOWNSTREAM DEPENDENCIES

These terms can become ambiguous unless the direction is stated clearly.

For this curriculum, always describe the relationship explicitly.

If:

```text
Service A
↓ calls
Service B
```

then Service A depends on Service B.

Rather than relying only on "upstream" and "downstream," state:

```text
A calls B.
B is a runtime dependency of A.
```

This removes ambiguity during incidents.

---

# 20. INBOUND DEPENDENCIES

Other systems may depend on your service.

For example:

```text
Web Frontend
↓
Orders API
```

If you own the Orders API, the frontend depends on your service.

A failure in your service can therefore propagate outward.

This matters when assessing impact.

---

# 21. OUTBOUND DEPENDENCIES

Your service may call:

```text
Database
Payment API
Cache
Identity Provider
Message Broker
```

These are runtime dependencies.

A useful service map records:

```text
Dependency

Purpose

Connection Method

Failure Effect

Timeout Behavior

Ownership

Recovery Expectations
```

Never place credentials in the map.

---

# 22. SYNCHRONOUS DEPENDENCIES

A synchronous dependency participates directly in the request path.

For example:

```text
User
↓
Application
↓
Database
↓
Application
↓
User
```

The application waits for the database operation before completing the request.

This creates direct latency and availability coupling.

If the database becomes slow, the application request may become slow.

---

# 23. SYNCHRONOUS FAILURE PROPAGATION

Consider:

```text
Database latency increases
↓
Application requests wait longer
↓
Workers remain occupied longer
↓
Request queue grows
↓
Application latency increases
↓
Timeouts increase
```

The application host may have healthy CPU and memory throughout the incident.

Without a dependency model, the wrong layer may be investigated.

---

# 24. ASYNCHRONOUS DEPENDENCIES

An asynchronous path separates work in time.

For example:

```text
Application
↓
Queue
↓
Worker
↓
Email Provider
```

The application may accept a request before the email is actually sent.

This changes failure behavior.

If the worker stops:

```text
Request accepted
↓
Message remains queued
↓
Backlog grows
↓
User-visible effect appears later
```

The service can therefore appear healthy while unfinished work accumulates.

---

# 25. QUEUE DEPTH IS PART OF SERVICE HEALTH

For asynchronous systems, health cannot be judged only from frontend request success.

Relevant evidence may include:

- queue depth
- message age
- consumer health
- processing rate
- retry rate
- dead-letter behavior

The exact signals depend on the system.

The important principle is:

```text
Accepted Work
≠
Completed Work
```

---

# 26. STATEFUL COMPONENTS

A stateful component preserves important information across requests or process restarts.

Examples may include:

- databases
- persistent queues
- object storage
- persistent volumes
- durable filesystems

State changes recovery requirements.

If a stateless application instance is destroyed, another instance may replace it.

If durable data is destroyed, replacing the process may not restore the service.

---

# 27. STATELESS COMPONENTS

A stateless service instance does not depend on unique local durable state to continue serving requests.

Conceptually:

```text
Instance A fails
↓
Instance B can serve equivalent requests
```

provided required external state and dependencies remain available.

Stateless does not mean:

```text
No state exists anywhere.
```

It often means state is stored outside the application instance.

---

# 28. WHY STATE CHANGES OPERATIONAL RISK

Consider two failures.

### Failure A

```text
Application Container Deleted
```

If the application is stateless and managed by an orchestrator, it may be recreated.

### Failure B

```text
Primary Database Data Deleted
```

Recovery may require:

- replica promotion
- backup restoration
- point-in-time recovery
- data validation
- application coordination

The blast radius is very different.

A service owner must recognize this difference before making changes.

---

# 29. EXTERNAL SERVICES

A service may depend on systems outside your direct administrative control.

Examples include:

- payment provider
- identity provider
- messaging provider
- external API
- managed SaaS platform

You may be unable to repair the external system.

You are still responsible for understanding how your service behaves when it fails.

Questions include:

```text
What is the timeout?

Are requests retried?

Could retries amplify the failure?

Does the application degrade gracefully?

What evidence identifies the external dependency?

What can be communicated to users?

What can be mitigated locally?
```

---

# 30. FAILURE DOMAINS

A failure domain is a boundary within which a failure can affect multiple components.

Examples depend on the environment but may include:

- process
- container
- host
- rack
- availability zone
- region
- cluster
- network segment
- storage system
- account
- shared dependency

The important question is:

> Which components can fail together because they share something?

---

# 31. SHARED DEPENDENCIES CREATE SHARED FAILURE

Suppose three services use the same database:

```text
Service A ─┐
Service B ─┼──→ Database
Service C ─┘
```

The services may appear independent.

Operationally, they share a failure domain.

If the database fails:

```text
Database Failure
↓
A affected
B affected
C affected
```

This matters when estimating incident scope.

---

# 32. APPARENT REDUNDANCY CAN SHARE A FAILURE DOMAIN

Consider:

```text
Application Instance A
Application Instance B
```

Two instances appear redundant.

But if both run on:

```text
Same Host
```

then host failure removes both.

Similarly, two instances in separate hosts may still share:

- one network path
- one storage backend
- one availability zone
- one database
- one credential
- one configuration error
- one deployment pipeline

Redundancy must be evaluated against the failure being considered.

---

# 33. BUILDING A SERVICE MAP

A service map is a structured representation of the components and relationships required for a service to operate.

It should help answer:

```text
What exists?

How is it connected?

What depends on what?

Who owns each component?

Where can it fail?

How is failure detected?

How is recovery performed?
```

The map does not need to begin as a sophisticated diagram.

Accuracy matters more than visual complexity.

---

# 34. START WITH THE USER PATH

Begin with the service's primary useful outcome.

For a web application:

```text
User
↓
DNS
↓
Entry Point
↓
Application
↓
Dependency
↓
Response
```

Then expand each component.

For example:

```text
User
↓
DNS
↓
Load Balancer
↓
Application Container
↓
Database
```

Then add the supporting delivery and operational paths.

---

# 35. ADD THE DELIVERY PATH

The runtime path explains how requests are served.

The delivery path explains how software reaches the runtime.

```text
Developer
↓
Git Repository
↓
Pull Request
↓
CI
↓
Build
↓
Container Registry
↓
Deployment
↓
Runtime Environment
```

Both belong in the service model.

---

# 36. ADD CONFIGURATION

Extend the model:

```text
Repository
    ↓
Build
    ↓
Artifact
    ↓
Deployment
    ↓
Runtime
    ↑
Configuration
```

Then identify the configuration source.

For example:

```text
Configuration Repository
        ↓
Deployment System
        ↓
Runtime Environment
```

or:

```text
Environment Configuration
        ↓
Runtime
```

The implementation depends on the service.

---

# 37. ADD SECRETS WITHOUT EXPOSING THEM

A service map may record:

```text
Application
↓
Database Credential
↓
Approved Secret Store
```

It must not record:

```text
Database Password = ...
```

The map should describe the dependency and delivery mechanism, not reveal the credential.

---

# 38. ADD OBSERVABILITY

Show where operational evidence comes from.

For example:

```text
Application
├── Logs
├── Metrics
└── Health Endpoint

Host
├── CPU Metrics
├── Memory Metrics
└── System Logs

Deployment
└── Pipeline History
```

This immediately improves troubleshooting.

A map should not only show:

```text
What can fail?
```

It should help answer:

```text
Where would evidence of that failure appear?
```

---

# 39. ADD RECOVERY

For important components, record the recovery mechanism.

Examples:

```text
Application Process
→ automatic restart

Application Instance
→ replacement

Bad Deployment
→ rollback or roll forward

Configuration Error
→ corrected configuration and redeployment

Database
→ platform-specific failover / restore procedure

Infrastructure
→ recreate from reviewed IaC where appropriate
```

Do not claim a recovery mechanism exists until it has been verified.

---

# 40. DOCUMENT UNKNOWN RECOVERY

If the database recovery procedure is unknown, write:

```text
Database Recovery:
Unknown. Confirm with database/platform owner.
```

Do not invent:

```text
Database Recovery:
Automatic failover
```

because the architecture appears highly available.

Unknown is better than false confidence.

---

# 41. OPERATIONAL MATURITY

A Junior question may be:

```text
Can I deploy it?
```

A Mid-Level question becomes:

```text
Can I operate it safely after deployment?
```

This changes the definition of "done."

---

# 42. DEPLOYED IS NOT OPERABLE

A service can be successfully deployed while lacking:

- useful logs
- metrics
- alerts
- health checks
- rollback procedure
- recovery procedure
- documentation
- dependency visibility
- ownership information

The application may technically run.

The operational maturity is still weak.

---

# 43. THE DAY-TWO QUESTION

Initial deployment is sometimes described as "day one."

Everything required afterward is often called "day two" operation.

The exact terminology is less important than the distinction:

```text
Build and Deploy
       ↓
Operate
       ↓
Observe
       ↓
Troubleshoot
       ↓
Patch
       ↓
Upgrade
       ↓
Scale
       ↓
Recover
       ↓
Improve
```

A production service spends far more time being operated than being deployed for the first time.

---

# 44. ASK OPERATIONAL QUESTIONS BEFORE DEPLOYMENT

Before production deployment, ask:

- How do we know the application started correctly?
- How do we know it is serving traffic?
- Where are logs?
- Which metrics matter?
- What alerts exist?
- What dependencies must be available?
- What happens if they are unavailable?
- What resources can become exhausted?
- How is configuration changed?
- How are credentials supplied?
- What happens if deployment fails?
- How do we roll back?
- How is state protected?
- Who should be contacted when another component fails?

These questions change deployment from an event into an operational plan.

---

# 45. AVAILABILITY

Availability concerns whether the service can provide its intended function when required.

A process existing does not prove availability.

A container running does not prove availability.

A VM responding to SSH does not prove availability.

A Kubernetes Pod in `Running` phase does not prove application availability.

Availability must be evaluated from the service's intended function.

---

# 46. PERFORMANCE

A service can be available and still perform poorly.

Examples include:

- slow requests
- high queue delay
- slow background processing
- database latency
- CPU saturation
- memory pressure
- storage latency
- dependency timeout

Therefore:

```text
Available
≠
Performing Acceptably
```

Performance expectations should be known well enough to recognize meaningful degradation.

---

# 47. SECURITY

Operational ownership includes security.

A service owner should understand:

- which identities access the service
- which identity the service itself uses
- where secrets originate
- which network exposure exists
- which permissions are granted
- which dependencies are trusted
- how software dependencies are introduced
- how container images or packages are obtained
- where audit evidence exists

Security is part of normal service operation.

---

# 48. OBSERVABILITY

You cannot reliably own a service that becomes invisible when something goes wrong.

Useful observability may include:

```text
Logs
+
Metrics
+
Traces
+
Events
+
Deployment History
+
Infrastructure Evidence
```

Not every service requires every possible telemetry source.

The important requirement is enough evidence to understand important service behavior.

---

# 49. DOCUMENTATION

Documentation is part of service ownership because operational knowledge cannot safely depend on one engineer's memory.

Useful service documentation may include:

- architecture overview
- repository location
- build procedure
- deployment process
- configuration sources
- dependency map
- health checks
- dashboards
- alerts
- common failure procedures
- rollback
- recovery
- ownership
- escalation contacts or process
- known limitations

Documentation should describe the actual system.

Outdated documentation can be more dangerous than missing documentation because it creates false confidence.

---

# 50. OPERATIONAL PROCEDURES

Some recurring operations should have documented procedures.

Examples include:

- deployment
- rollback
- credential rotation
- certificate renewal
- service restart
- scaling
- recovery
- maintenance
- incident escalation

A good procedure should include more than commands.

It should identify:

```text
Purpose

Prerequisites

Risk

Expected State

Action

Verification

Failure Handling

Escalation
```

---

# 51. RECOVERY

Recovery asks:

> What happens after something important fails?

Different components require different recovery approaches.

Examples:

```text
Failed Process
→ restart

Failed Stateless Instance
→ replacement

Bad Release
→ rollback / roll forward

Lost Configuration
→ restore from source of truth

Failed Infrastructure
→ recreate or fail over

Lost Data
→ restore or recover through verified data-protection mechanism
```

The exact recovery mechanism depends on the system.

---

# 52. RECOVERY MUST BE PROVABLE

A statement such as:

```text
We have backups.
```

does not prove:

```text
We can recover.
```

Similarly:

```text
We have a rollback button.
```

does not prove:

```text
Rollback will restore service.
```

Operational ownership asks for evidence.

At this level, the learner should begin recognizing the difference between **having a recovery mechanism** and **having confidence in recovery**.

Deeper disaster recovery engineering appears later in the Mid-Level path.

---

# 53. ENGINEERING TRADEOFFS

Production engineering rarely offers a perfect solution.

A change can improve one property while worsening another.

A Mid-Level engineer must begin evaluating tradeoffs systematically.

Core dimensions include:

- complexity
- reliability
- performance
- cost
- security
- maintainability
- operational burden
- recovery

---

# 54. COMPLEXITY

Every component creates something that must be understood and operated.

Adding:

- another database
- another queue
- another cluster
- another proxy
- another deployment system
- another cloud
- another abstraction

may solve a real problem.

It also introduces additional:

- configuration
- dependencies
- failure modes
- security boundaries
- upgrades
- monitoring
- troubleshooting
- documentation

Complexity should purchase something valuable.

---

# 55. ACCIDENTAL COMPLEXITY

Consider a small internal application with modest traffic.

Introducing:

```text
Kubernetes
+
Service Mesh
+
Multiple Clusters
+
Multiple Regions
+
Several Datastores
```

does not automatically make it production-grade.

It may create operational complexity that the requirements do not justify.

The engineering question is:

> Which requirement requires this complexity?

---

# 56. RELIABILITY

A design may improve reliability by introducing:

- redundancy
- health checking
- automatic replacement
- failover
- retry mechanisms
- multiple instances

But reliability mechanisms also have failure modes.

For example:

```text
Retry
```

can help with transient failure.

Uncontrolled retry behavior can also increase load on an already failing dependency.

Engineering decisions require context.

---

# 57. PERFORMANCE

A performance improvement may require:

- more compute
- caching
- concurrency
- different storage
- fewer network calls
- application optimization

Each has tradeoffs.

Caching, for example, may reduce dependency load while introducing:

- stale data
- invalidation complexity
- memory usage
- another operational component

Do not evaluate performance in isolation.

---

# 58. COST

The technically largest system is not automatically the best system.

Cost includes more than the infrastructure invoice.

Consider:

```text
Infrastructure Cost
+
Licensing
+
Operational Labor
+
Engineering Time
+
Support Burden
+
Incident Cost
```

A cheaper infrastructure design that creates constant incidents may be expensive operationally.

A more expensive managed service may reduce operational burden.

There is no universal answer.

---

# 59. SECURITY

Stronger controls may affect:

- developer convenience
- deployment speed
- operational workflows
- system complexity

That does not make security optional.

It means the implementation should satisfy security requirements without creating unnecessary friction or unsafe workarounds.

---

# 60. MAINTAINABILITY

Ask:

```text
Can another engineer understand this?

Can it be tested?

Can it be upgraded?

Can configuration be changed safely?

Is behavior documented?

Are dependencies understood?

Is the design unnecessarily clever?
```

A system that only one engineer understands is an operational risk.

---

# 61. OPERATIONAL BURDEN

Every design creates work after deployment.

Ask:

```text
Who patches it?

Who upgrades it?

Who monitors it?

Who responds when it fails?

Who rotates credentials?

Who maintains dashboards?

Who verifies backups?

Who updates documentation?
```

Operational burden belongs in design decisions.

---

# 62. RECOVERY TRADEOFFS

Recovery capability may require:

- replicas
- backups
- additional storage
- standby systems
- tested procedures
- automation

These have cost and complexity.

The correct question is not:

> What is the strongest possible recovery architecture?

It is:

> What recovery capability does this service require, and what design provides it with acceptable cost and operational complexity?

---

# 63. TRADEOFF EXAMPLE: ONE INSTANCE OR TWO

Requirement:

> Run an internal application.

Option A:

```text
One Instance
```

Advantages:

- simpler
- cheaper
- easier to understand

Disadvantages:

- instance failure can remove service

Option B:

```text
Two Instances
+
Traffic Distribution
```

Advantages:

- one instance may fail without total service loss

Disadvantages:

- more infrastructure
- more configuration
- more monitoring
- more cost
- application must support multiple instances correctly

The correct choice depends on requirements.

---

# 64. TRADEOFF EXAMPLE: SELF-MANAGED DATABASE OR MANAGED DATABASE

A self-managed database may provide:

- greater implementation control
- different cost characteristics
- customization

It also creates responsibility for areas such as:

- patching
- backups
- monitoring
- recovery
- replication
- upgrades

A managed database may move some operational responsibility to the provider while introducing:

- service constraints
- provider-specific behavior
- different costs
- platform dependency

The decision should be driven by requirements and constraints, not fashion.

---

# 65. A SIMPLE DECISION FRAMEWORK

For a meaningful implementation choice, record:

```text
Problem

Requirements

Constraints

Options

Benefits

Risks

Operational Impact

Recovery Impact

Decision

Verification
```

At Mid-Level, this does not need to become a lengthy architecture document for every small change.

The habit matters.

---

# 66. DO NOT START WITH THE TOOL

Weak reasoning:

```text
We should use Kubernetes.
```

Stronger reasoning:

```text
We need to run several independently deployable containerized services,
replace failed instances automatically, perform controlled rolling
deployments, and standardize workload operation across the team.

We should evaluate whether the existing Kubernetes platform satisfies
those requirements with acceptable operational complexity.
```

Start with the problem.

Then evaluate the technology.

---

# 67. RISK ASSESSMENT

Before a change, ask:

```text
What can this change affect?

How many users?

Which environment?

Which dependencies?

Is state involved?

Can the change be reversed?

How quickly?

What evidence will reveal failure?

Who needs to approve it?
```

Risk assessment should influence how the change is executed.

---

# 68. BLAST RADIUS

Blast radius describes the potential scope of impact if something goes wrong.

Examples:

```text
One Test Container
```

has a different blast radius from:

```text
Production Cluster
```

and:

```text
One Service Configuration
```

has a different blast radius from:

```text
Shared Network Routing
```

A Mid-Level engineer should deliberately reduce blast radius where practical.

---

# 69. REDUCING BLAST RADIUS

Possible techniques include:

- testing in a disposable environment
- using non-production first
- changing one instance before all instances
- using reviewed automation
- using limited permissions
- applying changes to a narrow target
- maintaining rollback capability

The exact technique depends on the system.

The principle is:

```text
Learn With the Smallest Reasonable Impact
```

---

# 70. SERVICE OWNERSHIP DURING INCIDENTS

During a service failure, ownership means more than attempting repairs.

The engineer should establish:

```text
What is failing?

Who is affected?

Which component appears unhealthy?

Which dependencies are involved?

What changed?

What evidence exists?

Who owns each relevant layer?

What can be mitigated safely?

What requires escalation?
```

The goal is coordinated restoration, not individual heroics.

---

# 71. LAYER OWNERSHIP

Consider:

```text
User
↓
DNS
↓
Load Balancer
↓
Kubernetes
↓
Pod
↓
Application
↓
Database
```

Different teams may own different layers.

For example:

| Layer | Possible Owner |
|---|---|
| Application | Application team |
| CI/CD | Platform or DevOps team |
| Kubernetes platform | Platform team |
| Network | Network or cloud team |
| Database | Database or data platform team |
| Identity | Security or identity team |

Actual ownership varies by organization.

Never assume these assignments universally.

The service owner should know the assignments for the environment being operated.

---

# 72. TROUBLESHOOTING ACROSS OWNERSHIP BOUNDARIES

Suppose the application reports database connection timeouts.

Weak escalation:

> Database is broken. Please fix.

Useful escalation:

> The Orders API began timing out database connections at 14:07 UTC. Application instances are healthy and continue accepting requests, but requests requiring the database fail. DNS resolution for the database endpoint succeeds. TCP connection attempts to the configured database endpoint time out from all three application instances. No application deployment occurred during the incident window. Could the database/network owner investigate availability of the database endpoint from the application network?

The second escalation provides:

- symptom
- timestamp
- scope
- evidence
- tests already performed
- affected dependency

That is service ownership even when another team performs the repair.

```text

```

# 74. AI AT MID-LEVEL

AI can participate in substantially more engineering work at Mid-Level.

Possible uses include:

- repository analysis
- configuration review
- infrastructure generation
- pipeline development
- troubleshooting
- log analysis
- test generation
- migration planning
- documentation
- incident analysis

The potential productivity gain increases.

So does the potential blast radius.

The relationship is:

```text
More Powerful AI Assistance
↓
More Potential Change
↓
Greater Verification Requirement
↓
Stronger Guardrails
```

---

# 75. AI CAN ANALYZE A REPOSITORY

With approved access and appropriate tooling, AI may help identify:

- application structure
- build files
- infrastructure code
- pipeline definitions
- configuration files
- tests
- deployment manifests
- documentation

This can accelerate initial understanding.

It does not prove the AI's interpretation is correct.

Verify important claims against the repository itself.

---

# 76. AI CAN MISS RUNTIME REALITY

Suppose repository configuration says:

```text
PORT=8080
```

AI may conclude:

> The production application listens on port 8080.

That conclusion is not yet proven.

Production might override the value.

The correct chain is:

```text
Repository Configuration
↓
Possible Intended State
↓
Inspect Deployment Configuration
↓
Inspect Runtime Environment
↓
Inspect Actual Listener
↓
Verified Runtime State
```

Repository analysis and production verification are different forms of evidence.

---

# 77. AI-GENERATED INFRASTRUCTURE

AI can generate Terraform or other infrastructure definitions.

Before accepting generated infrastructure, inspect:

- resources
- provider/version assumptions
- network exposure
- permissions
- state implications
- dependencies
- naming
- lifecycle behavior
- destructive actions
- cost implications
- environment compatibility

Then use the tool's own validation and planning mechanisms.

AI-generated infrastructure is proposed code.

It is not approved infrastructure.

---

# 78. AI-GENERATED PIPELINES

AI can generate CI/CD configuration quickly.

Review:

- triggers
- permissions
- secrets
- build steps
- tests
- artifacts
- deployment targets
- environment protections
- third-party dependencies
- failure handling
- verification
- rollback behavior

A pipeline can be syntactically correct and operationally unsafe.

---

# 79. AI-ASSISTED TROUBLESHOOTING

AI can help generate hypotheses.

The correct model remains:

```text
Evidence
↓
AI-Assisted Hypotheses
↓
Human Evaluation
↓
Safe Test
↓
New Evidence
↓
Conclusion
```

Not:

```text
Error Message
↓
AI Answer
↓
Production Command
```

---

# 80. AI-ASSISTED LOG ANALYSIS

AI may help summarize large log samples or identify patterns.

Before sharing logs, consider:

- credentials
- tokens
- customer information
- internal addresses
- proprietary data
- organizational policy
- approved AI tooling

After receiving an analysis, verify important claims against the original evidence.

Summarization can omit details.

---

# 81. AI-GENERATED TESTS

AI can help generate:

- unit tests
- integration tests
- negative tests
- configuration tests
- deployment verification

Review whether the tests actually verify the required behavior.

A generated test that always passes provides false confidence.

---

# 82. AI-ASSISTED MIGRATION PLANNING

AI may help enumerate:

- migration stages
- dependencies
- compatibility questions
- rollback considerations
- testing requirements
- data movement concerns

Migration planning requires verified knowledge of the actual environment.

Never allow an AI-generated migration plan to invent system assumptions silently.

---

# 83. AI AND INCIDENT ANALYSIS

AI can help:

- organize a timeline
- correlate known events
- summarize logs
- identify missing evidence
- propose hypotheses
- draft incident documentation

It must not transform correlation into unsupported causation.

For example:

```text
Deployment occurred at 10:00
Errors began at 10:02
```

supports investigating the deployment.

It does not, by itself, prove:

```text
Deployment caused the incident.
```

---

# 84. AI AUTHORITY MUST BE BOUNDED

An AI tool that can only explain text has a smaller operational blast radius than an AI agent that can:

```text
Read Repository
↓
Modify Terraform
↓
Run Terraform
↓
Change Production
```

As tool authority increases, controls must increase.

Consider:

- read versus write access
- development versus production access
- sandboxing
- command restrictions
- approval gates
- credential scope
- audit logs
- rollback
- human review

---

# 85. INTELLIGENCE AND AUTHORITY ARE DIFFERENT

An AI system may be capable of proposing a sophisticated change.

That does not mean it should be authorized to execute the change.

Keep separate:

```text
What the AI can reason about
```

from:

```text
What the AI is permitted to change
```

This distinction becomes increasingly important as agentic tooling becomes more capable.

# 104. FAILURE SCENARIO: DNS

Observed:

```text
Application is healthy when accessed directly.

Normal service hostname does not resolve correctly.
```

Before remediation determine:

- which DNS record is involved
- expected value
- observed value
- DNS owner
- whether you are authorized to change it
- blast radius of changing it

Do not modify DNS merely because the application team owns the service.

---

# 105. FAILURE SCENARIO: ENTRY POINT

Observed:

```text
DNS resolves correctly.

Application instances are healthy.

Requests fail at the load balancer or ingress layer.
```

Determine:

- entry-point owner
- target health
- routing configuration
- recent changes
- available logs/metrics

The application may not own the failing component.

The service owner still coordinates the investigation.

---

# 106. FAILURE SCENARIO: APPLICATION

Observed:

```text
Network path reaches the host/runtime.

Application process repeatedly exits.

Application logs show invalid configuration.
```

The relevant owner is likely closer to the application/configuration layer.

Investigate the actual ownership model rather than escalating to networking because users reported "connection failure."

---

# 107. FAILURE SCENARIO: DATABASE

Observed:

```text
Application is running.

Requests that do not require the database succeed.

Database-backed requests fail.

Application logs show database connection failures.
```

The evidence narrows the investigation.

Determine:

- configured endpoint
- DNS result
- network reachability where appropriate
- authentication result
- database availability evidence
- database owner

Do not claim:

```text
Database is down.
```

until the evidence supports that conclusion.

---

# 108. FAILURE SCENARIO: EXPIRED CREDENTIAL

Observed:

```text
Application reaches dependency.

Dependency rejects authentication.

Credential is expired or no longer accepted.
```

The network is functioning.

The process is functioning.

The failure belongs to the identity/credential path.

The service owner should know:

- credential source
- rotation process
- authorized owner
- deployment/reload requirements after rotation

Never expose the credential during troubleshooting.

---

# 109. FAILURE SCENARIO: PIPELINE

Observed:

```text
Production remains healthy.

New deployment cannot proceed.

Pipeline fails during artifact publication.
```

This is a service-delivery failure, not necessarily a runtime outage.

Ownership may involve:

- application repository
- CI/CD platform
- artifact registry
- identity/permissions

Correctly identifying the failure domain prevents unnecessary production changes.

---

# 110. ESCALATION IS PART OF OWNERSHIP

Escalation is not failure.

Good ownership includes knowing when the problem exceeds:

- your access
- your authority
- your expertise
- your service boundary
- your acceptable risk

A useful escalation contains:

```text
Symptom

Impact

Start Time

Scope

Expected State

Observed State

Evidence

Actions Already Taken

Relevant Changes

Current Hypothesis

Specific Assistance Required
```

This allows the receiving engineer to continue the investigation rather than restart it.

---

# 111. DO NOT ESCALATE A CONCLUSION AS IF IT WERE EVIDENCE

Weak:

> The network is broken.

Better:

> Requests from the application hosts to the dependency endpoint time out. DNS resolves to the expected address. No TCP connection is established from either application host. Other outbound connections from those hosts succeed.

The second statement provides evidence.

The network owner can investigate from there.

# 112. SERVICE OWNERSHIP AND IMPROVEMENT

Mid-Level ownership does not stop after an incident is resolved.

Ask:

```text
What made this difficult to detect?

What made it difficult to diagnose?

What made it difficult to recover?

Which manual step was error-prone?

Which documentation was missing?

Which telemetry was missing?

Which guardrail would prevent recurrence?
```

This creates the final step of the Mid-Level model:

```text
Requirement / Problem
↓
Understand System
↓
Assess Risk
↓
Choose Implementation
↓
Execute
↓
Verify
↓
Observe
↓
Improve
```

---

# 113. IMPROVEMENT DOES NOT MEAN AUTOMATE EVERYTHING

Automation is useful when it reduces:

- repetition
- inconsistency
- human error
- recovery time
- operational burden

But automation also creates:

- code
- permissions
- dependencies
- maintenance
- failure modes

Automate deliberately.

A manual process performed twice is not automatically a platform requirement.

---

# 114. IMPROVEMENT SHOULD ADDRESS EVIDENCE

Suppose an incident took 40 minutes to diagnose because nobody knew which application version was running.

A useful improvement might be:

```text
Expose and record deployment version identity.
```

Suppose diagnosis was delayed because dependency latency was invisible.

A useful improvement might be:

```text
Add dependency latency telemetry.
```

Improvements should address demonstrated operational weaknesses.

# 115. THE MID-LEVEL SERVICE-OWNER CHECKLIST

For a service you own, you should progressively be able to answer the following.

### Purpose

- What does the service do?
- Who depends on it?
- What business or technical function does it provide?

### Source

- Where is the source?
- How are changes reviewed?
- Which revision is deployed?

### Build

- How is the artifact built?
- What tests run?
- Where is the artifact stored?

### Configuration

- What configuration is required?
- Where is it defined?
- How does it reach runtime?

### Secrets

- Which secret types are required?
- Where are they supplied from?
- Who owns rotation?

### Deployment

- How is deployment triggered?
- Which environments exist?
- How is deployment verified?
- How is rollback performed?

### Infrastructure

- Where does the service run?
- Which infrastructure does it depend on?
- Which parts are shared?

### Runtime

- Which process or workload represents the service?
- Which ports does it use?
- What identity does it run as?
- What constitutes healthy runtime behavior?

### Dependencies

- What does the service call?
- What calls the service?
- Which dependencies are synchronous?
- Which are asynchronous?
- Which are stateful?

### Availability

- What failures make the service unavailable?
- What redundancy exists?
- Which shared failure domains remain?

### Performance

- What does normal performance look like?
- Which resources or dependencies commonly constrain it?

### Security

- Which identities and permissions matter?
- Which secrets exist?
- Which network exposure is intentional?

### Observability

- Where are logs?
- Which metrics matter?
- Which alerts exist?
- How are recent changes identified?

### Recovery

- How is a bad deployment recovered?
- How is a failed instance recovered?
- How is important state recovered?
- Which recovery mechanisms have actually been tested?

### Documentation

- Could another engineer operate the service without relying on your memory?

If the answer to the last question is no, ownership is incomplete.

# 117. FAILURE INVESTIGATION ASSESSMENT

After the service map is complete, introduce a failure.

Do not tell the learner which layer contains the root cause.

The learner must produce:

```text
Symptom

Impact

Scope

Expected State

Observed State

Service Path

Suspected Layer

Layer Owner

Evidence

Hypotheses

Safe Tests

Root Cause or Escalation Point

Corrective Action if Authorized

Verification
```

A passing result does not require the learner to personally repair every possible component.

It requires the learner to demonstrate that they can locate the problem within the service model and hand off useful evidence when another owner is required.

# 118. TRADEOFF ASSESSMENT

Provide a requirement such as:

> The service currently runs as one application instance. Short outages occur whenever the host is unavailable. The team wants to reduce this risk.

The learner should not immediately answer:

```text
Use Kubernetes.
```

They should identify:

- availability requirement
- current failure domain
- possible options
- complexity
- cost
- operational burden
- deployment implications
- state considerations
- recovery implications

The objective is to demonstrate decision reasoning.

# 120. COMPLETION STANDARD

You have completed this Part when you can explain what meaningful production service ownership involves and clearly distinguish execution responsibility from engineering ownership.

You should be able to move from:

```text
Tell me what command to run.
```

toward:

```text
What problem are we solving?

How does this service actually work?

Which components participate?

What are the dependencies?

What can fail?

Who owns each layer?

What evidence do we have?

What implementation is appropriate?

What risk does the change introduce?

How will we test it?

How will we deploy it?

How will we verify it?

How will we observe it?

How will we recover?

What should we improve afterward?
```

You should understand that service ownership includes responsibility for reasoning about:

- source
- build
- configuration
- deployment
- infrastructure
- runtime
- dependencies
- availability
- performance
- security
- observability
- documentation
- operational procedures
- recovery

You should be able to create a service map that explains:

```text
How software reaches production

How users reach the service

How the service reaches its dependencies

Where configuration and secrets enter the system

Where operational evidence comes from

Where failures can propagate

Who owns each relevant layer

How important failures are recovered
```

You should also understand that ownership does not grant unlimited authority.

A production service owner must know when to:

```text
Investigate
↓
Collect Evidence
↓
Act Within Authority
```

and when to:

```text
Investigate
↓
Collect Evidence
↓
Escalate With Useful Context
```

The Mid-Level engineering habit established in this Part is:

```text
Requirement / Problem
        ↓
Understand the Service
        ↓
Understand the Dependencies
        ↓
Understand the Failure Domains
        ↓
Understand Ownership Boundaries
        ↓
Assess Risk
        ↓
Evaluate Tradeoffs
        ↓
Choose a Bounded Implementation
        ↓
Execute Safely
        ↓
Verify the Intended Outcome
        ↓
Observe the Service
        ↓
Recover if Necessary
        ↓
Improve the System
```

The transition from Junior to Mid-Level is not completed by learning more tools.

It is completed when you stop seeing production as a collection of commands and begin seeing it as a system for which engineering decisions have consequences.
